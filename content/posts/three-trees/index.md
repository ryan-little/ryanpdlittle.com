The tallest tree on Earth is somewhere in a stretch of fog-wet forest in Northern California, and the Park Service will not tell you which one it is. You can hike near it, you can stand a few hundred feet from it, you can probably photograph it without realizing, but they stopped giving out the coordinates years ago because hikers were trampling the root system trying to find it. The same thing is true for the oldest tree, a bristlecone pine up in the White Mountains, where Methuselah stands unmarked in a grove of other ancient trees that all look about the same level of battered. The largest tree is the exception. General Sherman sits behind a fence in Sequoia National Park with a boardwalk around it and a sign telling you exactly what you're looking at, because when a tree is that big, hiding it is not really an option.

I saw all three across four years, on three separate trips, without planning for it to turn into a thing. It turned into a thing anyway, and what follows is tallest, largest, oldest, which is not the order I saw them in.

## Hyperion, August 2020

Hyperion is a coastal redwood, *Sequoia sempervirens*, and at 380 feet it's the tallest known living tree on the planet. It was only discovered in 2006, by two naturalists named Chris Atkins and Michael Taylor who were poking around an unmapped drainage in Redwood National Park with a laser rangefinder. A tree that tall had been standing there the whole time, and no one had measured it. That's the part I find hardest to get over, that in 2006 we were still finding new tallest-ever trees in California.

I went up there in August of 2020 with my college roommates, the summer where the world had mostly stopped and a road trip up the 101 was the closest thing to a real vacation any of us were going to get. We did Avenue of the Giants and Tall Trees Grove and they were both worth the drive on their own, but the day we went for Hyperion was the one that stuck.

![The forest floor beneath a coastal redwood in Redwood National Park, looking up through the canopy.](hyperion.webp)

The official line is that nobody really sees Hyperion. The coordinates are withheld, the area around it is closed to off-trail hiking, and the Park Service would prefer you walk a maintained loop somewhere and admire the general neighborhood. We went and found it anyway. Getting there meant crossing Redwood Creek, which was freezing and ran shallow over rocks slick enough that I spent the whole crossing watching for what I was pretty sure were leeches, or at least what I was choosing to call leeches at the time. The forest on the far side surprised me by being warm and dry and unusually quiet, the kind of quiet you only get when the ground is soft enough to absorb sound, ferns everywhere you looked and the trees so tall the canopy was somewhere else entirely.

People online will tell you that you can't tell which tree is Hyperion even when you're standing in front of it, and that is a lie. We rounded a corner after a long stretch of GPS-checking, each waypoint convincing me we were closer than we were, and there was no doubt about it. Hyperion is visibly larger than every tree around it, and every tree around it is already in the 350-foot range. Reaching it after the creek and the slick rocks and the false-near GPS readings was the old-school definition of awesome, the kind that means the thing in front of you is large enough to make you feel small.

## General Sherman, July 2020

Sequoia National Park sits up in the southern Sierra at around 7,000 feet, a different California entirely from the coastal redwoods. The giant sequoias there are a completely different species, *Sequoiadendron giganteum* instead of *Sequoia sempervirens*, shorter but immensely thicker. General Sherman is the headliner, the largest tree on Earth by volume, 275 feet tall with a base diameter of 36.5 feet and an estimated weight of 2.7 million pounds. It has been standing in that grove for roughly 2,200 years.

The drive up from San Diego to Sequoia is long, most of a day on I-5 and then the climb up the 198 into the park, and by the time you get to the Giant Forest the road has been twisting through switchbacks for an hour. We did the trip in July 2020 with the same college roommates I'd end up in Redwood Creek with a month later, except we didn't know that yet. The Sherman Tree trail is short, about half a mile downhill to the tree and then the same half mile back up, which is a small reminder at 7,000 feet that you are not going to breeze through it.

![General Sherman, the largest tree on Earth by volume, in Sequoia National Park.](general-sherman.webp)

We made the mistake of swinging by General Grant first, the second-largest tree on Earth by volume, which sits up the road in Kings Canyon. Grant is enormous in its own right, and seeing it before Sherman is the kind of thing that should dampen the headliner. It didn't. By the time we got down to Sherman the line of people waiting to take a photograph in front of the fence stretched back along the path, telling you which tree was Sherman more efficiently than any sign.

A photograph of General Sherman does not work. The tree is too big to fit in a frame from any reasonable distance, and standing next to it the scale is so far beyond what your brain is used to that it stops registering as a tree and starts registering as a feature of the landscape, like a cliff face or a butte. The bark is a warm orange-red and fibrous, soft-looking up close, and the lowest branches start something like a hundred feet off the ground. Sequoias don't have branches where other trees have branches, because the equivalent altitude on a normal tree is the canopy. For the rest of that trip every tree I saw I sized up against Sherman, and the answer was always a small fraction.

What stuck with me from that trip wasn't actually Sherman. It was the drive through the sequoia-dense parts of the park and the meadows around the grove, the parts of Sequoia where you see the giant sequoias from a distance instead of from a fenced boardwalk. The grove is full of trees in the 1,500 to 2,000 year range, any of which would be the most impressive tree anywhere else. Sherman pulls the crowds. The grove around it is what stayed with me.

## Methuselah, July 2024

Four years later I finally got up to the White Mountains, which sit just east of the Sierra across the Owens Valley from Bishop, and which contain a grove of bristlecone pines that includes Methuselah. Methuselah is around 4,857 years old as of 2024, confirmed by a core sample taken in 1957 by a researcher named Edmund Schulman, which makes it one of the oldest known living non-clonal organisms on Earth. It was alive before the pyramids were built and before written language was standardized in most of the world, and it is still alive now.

The Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest is a different kind of place from the redwoods or the sequoias. The trailhead sits above 10,000 feet in elevation, the air is thin, the terrain is exposed and rocky with sparse vegetation, and the trees are twisted, gnarled things that look more dead than alive. That look is the point. Bristlecones survive by not growing much. A tree might be six feet in diameter and fifty feet tall after five thousand years of adding rings so thin you can barely see them without a hand lens. Slow growth means dense wood, dense wood means resistance to rot and insects, and resistance to rot means you get to keep being a tree for a very long time.

![An ancient bristlecone pine in the White Mountains, one of the oldest living organisms on Earth.](methuselah.webp)

The Methuselah Trail is a 4.3 mile loop through the grove, and somewhere along it Methuselah stands, unlabeled and unmarked, because the Forest Service made the same call the Park Service made with Hyperion. If they tell you which one it is, somebody is eventually going to do something to it. So you walk the loop and you know that one of the trees you passed, probably several of the trees you passed, is older than recorded history. In 2009 a researcher named Tom Harlan reported finding an even older tree in the same grove, around 5,062 years based on a 1957 core sample, but after Harlan died in 2013 the sample and the tree's location were both lost. So somewhere in that grove there is a tree even older than Methuselah, anonymous now in the most permanent sense.

July 2024 was my third trip up White Mountain Road. The first time, with a friend, we got snowed in on the climb and pulled out by a couple of guys in a sedan who came up behind us, one of whom turned out to be Ted Nivison the YouTuber, which is the kind of thing that happens once. The second time was with an ex, and we did the Methuselah Loop and saw the tree but I couldn't have told you which one it was. The third time I went with my mom and my little brother and we went looking specifically. You can find Methuselah by the roots if you know to look, the root structure noticeably larger and longer than any of its neighbors, and that's how we finally picked it out.

The drive up is part of why I keep going back. Highway 168 climbs out of the Owens Valley through Westgard Pass and the road keeps going up after that, and the Sierra View Overlook near the top has one of the best views of the Eastern Sierras you can get from a paved pullout. The hike from the trailhead isn't hard by distance or elevation gain, and the altitude is manageable if you remember to drink more water than you think you need. The landscape is austere in a way that fits the trees perfectly, dry and windswept and cold even in July, a place where slow growth is the only viable strategy. The bristlecones look like what you would design a tree to look like if you needed it to survive for millennia on very little.

What I keep coming back to about Methuselah is that it's one of the oldest living things I have ever laid eyes on, possibly the oldest, alive since around the time the pyramids were going up and still alive now. I have car-camped at a site up there before, and it's where I plan to stay for a couple of nights this August before heading over to Whitney, and it is strange to know that the trees up the hill from where I will be sleeping have been there since the Bronze Age.

## Why Three

There was no plan when I went to Sequoia in July 2020. General Sherman was the reason to go, the same way Old Faithful is the reason to go to Yellowstone, a thing you check off because it is the thing. The Redwoods a month later was where it became a project, a second trip up the 101 specifically to find Hyperion off-trail. Methuselah took longer than that, three separate trips before I could pick it out, and somewhere across those years I realized I was collecting superlatives, that I had already seen the largest and the tallest without meaning to, and that the oldest had been waiting up in the White Mountains the whole time.

The three trees are all within a day's drive of each other, all in California, all in protected public land, and all in the care of federal agencies that have collectively decided that two of them have to stay anonymous for their own safety. That seems right to me. The agencies want Hyperion and Methuselah to exist as facts more than as objects, and for most people walking the loop they do. You read the signs, you walk the trails, you know the trees are in there somewhere, and that is the experience the policy is designed to give you. General Sherman is the exception because at 2.7 million pounds anonymity is not available.

Part of what I liked about seeing all three is that they are so different from each other. Hyperion is all height, 380 feet of coastal redwood reaching for the fog. General Sherman is all volume, a tree-shaped mountain with bark the color of sunset. Methuselah is neither, a fifty-foot scrap of a tree that looks like it should not still be alive but has been outlasting everything around it since the Bronze Age. Three different ways of being a remarkable tree, three different landscapes, three different reasons to make the drive.

There's more on all three, with full stats and additional photos, over on [ryan-little.com/trees](https://ryan-little.com/trees). The blog is where I keep the stories, and the portfolio site is where I keep the record. Both pages will keep growing, because there are more trees on the list. The Major Oak in Sherwood Forest is on it. The dragon blood trees of Socotra are on it. The baobabs in Madagascar are on it. I'm going to take a while getting to those, but I've been working through the California list for four years, and with Methuselah I finally finished the big three.

Four years, several trips, one state, three trees I will not forget.
