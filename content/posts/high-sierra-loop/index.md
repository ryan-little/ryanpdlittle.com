The Half Dome cables are two parallel steel cables bolted into a 45-degree slab of granite for the last 400 vertical feet of the summit push, and the Park Service puts them up around Memorial Day weekend and takes them down in October. You pull yourself up between them, hand over hand, with your feet on wooden slats that are spaced roughly where your legs want to be and sometimes not. There's no way to do Half Dome without going through the cables, and there's no way to go through the cables without, at some point, looking down. I was in a group of friends in June of 2022, permits in hand, and when my turn came I clipped my gloves around a cable and started up.

That week was a loop through three different ranges in California, all in one trip. Yosemite and Half Dome to start, then south down the eastern side of the Sierra through Sequoia and Kings Canyon, and finally east across the Owens Valley into the White Mountains for the bristlecone pines. Three parks, three elevation bands, one rental car, about a week of driving and hiking and camping between them. It was the biggest trip I had taken in California up to that point, and most of what I remember about it is the way the landscape kept changing underneath the car.

## Half Dome

Half Dome is the granite monolith at the east end of Yosemite Valley, the one on the California state quarter, and the standard route to the top is a 14 to 16 mile round trip with about 4,800 feet of elevation gain depending on which trail configuration you use. We came up from the valley on the Mist Trail past Vernal and Nevada Falls, then joined the John Muir Trail for the long grind up to the subdome, and from there you get the first real look at what the cables actually are. The slab is steep enough that from the base it genuinely looks like a wall, and you can see the tiny shapes of people pulling themselves up it the whole time you're walking toward it.

The subdome itself is the harder section for me, which surprised me. It's a set of stone steps carved into the granite that goes up at a grade that never quite lets your legs recover, and by the time you reach the base of the cables you've already done a full day of climbing. The cables are mostly upper body and nerve from there. It took me longer than I expected to get up, not because any single move was hard, but because the angle of the slab does something to your sense of what is a reasonable thing to be doing. I kept making myself stop at a slat to let my grip rest, and every time I did I had to consciously not look over my shoulder at the valley floor 4,000 feet below.

The summit is broad and flat and it fools you, because there's nothing up there that suggests how narrow the thing you just climbed actually was. You walk around on the back of the dome for a while, find a spot to sit, eat whatever's still edible in your pack, and look across at Clouds Rest and the Tenaya Canyon drainage. The going down is its own problem. Going down the cables is where most accidents happen, because your center of gravity wants to put you off balance and gravity is now working with you instead of against you. I took my time, and the slab felt longer on the way down than it had on the way up.

## Sequoia and Kings Canyon, the Second Time

Two years earlier, in July of 2020, I had driven up to Sequoia specifically for General Sherman, and the trip I wrote about in the [three trees post](/posts/three-trees/) was that one. In June of 2022 I was back in the Giant Forest, which was a different experience, because the second time I was not there for the headliner. I had already seen General Sherman. I had already been recalibrated on what the word big meant for a tree. This time the grove was a stop on the way through, not a destination, and that changed what I paid attention to.

The first time you walk up on General Sherman, the scale does a thing to your brain that is hard to reproduce. The second time you walk up on it, the tree is still enormous, but you are ready for it, and the strangeness comes from looking around at everything else. The sequoias in the 1,500 to 2,000 year range, which on a first visit read as supporting cast, become the thing you actually look at. You walk up the Congress Trail or the trails around the Senate Grove, and you notice the bark on trees that do not have a plaque and do not have a boardwalk, and you start understanding the forest as a forest rather than as a setting for one tree.

Kings Canyon is the other half of the joint park unit, and it's the piece that most people who come for Sequoia never see, because you have to drive the spur road down into the canyon itself to get there. The road drops something like 4,000 feet from the rim into the South Fork Kings River drainage, and the canyon walls close in until you're in a steep granite valley that feels more like Yosemite than it does like the giant forest you just left. Zumwalt Meadow at the bottom is flat and green and ringed by cliffs, and Roads End is the literal end of the paved road and the start of some of the longest backcountry trails in the Sierra.

June in the Sierra is still shoulder-season for some of the higher country. The snowpack was melting, the rivers were loud with it, and the lower elevations were green in a way they would not be by August. The drive from the Giant Forest down into Kings Canyon and back out to Highway 395 is long, and by the end of it the car had climbed and dropped thousands of feet of elevation three or four times in a single day.

## The White Mountains

The Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest sits at about 10,000 feet in the White Mountains, which are the range that runs along the east side of the Owens Valley opposite the Sierra. If you stand at Schulman Grove on a clear day, you can look west and see the eastern escarpment of the Sierra, including the summit of Mount Whitney, across the valley. It is one of the few places on earth where you can be in a grove of trees that predate the pyramids and look out at the highest point in the lower 48 in the same glance.

I had been to General Sherman. I had been to Hyperion's forest. In June of 2022 I had not yet been among the bristlecones, and I did not know that two years later I would come back for the Methuselah Trail specifically and write about it as the third of the three trees. What I knew in 2022 was that this stop was the odd one out on the loop — a range I had never seen, at an elevation I had not hiked at much, looking at a kind of tree that does not exist anywhere else in California. The altitude was the first thing I noticed. 10,000 feet is high enough that a short walk feels like effort, and the bristlecones live there because nothing else really can.

Bristlecone wood is dense and the trees grow slowly, which is the reason they last. Most of what you see when you walk through the grove is bleached gray dead wood still attached to a living tree, because the cambium, the living layer under the bark, retreats to a single strip up one side of the trunk while the rest of the trunk dies but does not rot. A bristlecone can be ninety percent dead and still be the same tree. The surviving strip keeps a few branches going, and those branches put out needles, and the needles photosynthesize, and the tree keeps on being a tree for another few centuries. Standing in a grove of them, the landscape is more stone and wind than anything green. The trees match the place.

## The Drive

What I remember most about that week was not any single stop. It was the transitions. Yosemite Valley is lush and shaded and loud with waterfall noise in June. The eastern Sierra is drier and higher, the air thinner, the trees smaller. The Owens Valley between the Sierra and the Whites is one of the strangest landscapes in California, a desert floor with 14,000 foot peaks on both sides. And then the White Mountains on the other side are alpine in a way that has nothing to do with lushness, open and rocky and cold. Driving through that kind of terrain change in a single day is a reminder that California is not one landscape. It's ten of them, stacked against each other along a couple of long north-south corridors, and the High Sierra Loop is a way to visit three of them without leaving the state.

I came home from the trip more curious about the eastern Sierra than I had been going in. Whitney was right there, visible from Schulman Grove, a summit I had mostly thought about as a far-off thing. The bristlecones gave me an easy afternoon at 10,000 feet and the honest information that altitude was going to be the piece of Whitney that I would have to take seriously. And Half Dome had, in its own way, told me something about what sustained exposure and sustained climbing on granite actually feels like. I didn't know yet that I was going to build a training stack around a Whitney attempt, but the week had already put the idea within reach.

The cables come down in October. The bristlecones have been up there for five thousand years. Whitney was across the valley the whole time.
