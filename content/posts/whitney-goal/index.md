El Cajon Mountain is the hardest of the San Diego County peaks, and the first trail this year that left my legs genuinely worked the next morning. 11.3 miles, 4,144 feet of gain, nothing above 3,648 feet summit elevation. Whitney is 22 miles and 6,100 feet and tops out at 14,505. Those two numbers next to each other — El Cajon is less than half the elevation gain and less than half the distance — give you a feel for where the progression starts and how far it goes. The goal came from the Eastern Sierra trip I wrote about [here](/posts/high-sierra-loop), standing in the range and knowing Whitney was the highest point in the lower 48. El Cajon made it feel like a real problem to plan around.

## A List I Can't Skip Ahead On

The list I built to cover that distance splits into two tiers. The first is San Diego County: peaks I can drive to in under an hour, trails I can repeat whenever training calls for it. Cowles Mountain anchors this tier, the home trail, the one I've hiked nineteen times since April of last year and know well enough to track fitness by how my legs feel on the climb without checking the watch. Iron Mountain, Pyles Peak, Cuyamaca, Stonewall Peak, and El Cajon round out the county side. These are where distance and terrain confidence get built, where the repetition is the point.

The second tier is the Six-Pack of Peaks: six named SoCal summits chosen in order of increasing altitude, from Wilson at 5,710 feet up through three separate 10,000-plus peaks and finally to Gorgonio at 11,503. Each one is further from home, harder to permit, and higher than the last. Each one gets its own post. The same principle runs across both tiers: every trail teaches something the next one assumes you already have.

There are a few peaks from the original list that will likely slip to after Whitney — Fortuna Loop, Otay Mountain, Cactus to Clouds. The schedule turned out to be fuller than I expected when I first mapped it.

The temptation is always to skip ahead. Baldy sounds more interesting than another Cowles lap. Cactus to Clouds has a great name. But skipping steps means showing up to a harder trail without the thing it assumed you already built, and I'm close enough to the hard part of the list that the cost of that is no longer abstract.

## What's Been Done

The county tier is mostly behind me. Cowles, Iron Mountain, and Pyles are all checked. El Cajon happened in late April. Cuyamaca and Stonewall went on the same day about a week after Wilson, 11.3 miles total across both peaks, the first time I'd chained two summits in one outing. Stonewall made sense while I was already there, a shorter loop from the same park, and the legs had enough left to do it.

The Six-Pack started with Wilson in early May via the Sierra Madre trail, which came out to 16.4 miles and 5,203 feet of gain. Until today that was my longest day on foot or close to it. Beautiful day, entirely uphill on the way out, telescopes at the summit worth looking around. On the way back down I walked past a search and rescue operation without knowing what it was, and found out later that afternoon that someone had a heart attack near the summit and didn't survive. That information lands differently after you've just finished the same climb.

## What's Coming

From Cucamonga the Six-Pack continues through San Jacinto, San Bernardino Peak, Baldy, and Gorgonio in that order, each with its own post. Before the first full San Jacinto ascent, there's a tram day planned as a passive altitude probe: the Palm Springs aerial tram does the first 8,000 feet of climbing and drops you at 8,516, so you find out what 10,834 feet feels like before you've had to earn it up the full vert. Low-cost way to answer the question early.

Then San Jacinto by trail from Marion Mountain, the full aerobic ascent, 4,500 feet from a 6,500-foot trailhead. Then San Bernardino Peak, which is the longest pre-Whitney day on the calendar at 15.6 miles, and the closest thing to a Whitney mileage day before August. Then Baldy to keep altitude in range through July. Then Gorgonio: 17.3 miles, 5,500 feet of gain, 11,503-foot summit, three weeks before the permit date. If Gorgonio goes cleanly, Whitney becomes a shorter, higher version of something I've already done.

Gap-fillers in the off-weeks include a loop in the Angeles Crest, El Cajon again for descent repetition, and a Crystal Lake PCT route that is 20 miles and 5,700 feet, specifically planned as a Whitney long-day simulator.

Whitney is August 23rd on a day-use permit from the April lottery, group of three.

## Altitude Is Not the Problem I Expected

I've been at altitude enough times to know my pattern. Living in Fort Collins put me at 5,000 feet as a daily baseline. Hiking in Rocky Mountain National Park — including Lost Lake and up around Trail Ridge Road near 12,000 feet — gave me time at real elevation. The Eastern Sierra trip has altitude baked in, and so does Sequoia. The Methuselah Grove hike in the White Mountains, which I wrote about in [the Three Trees post](/posts/three-trees), sits around 10,000 feet. Across all of it my response has been faster dehydration and the occasional mild headache, nothing that stopped me or materially slowed me down.

So the altitude question for Whitney is not whether I handle elevation in general. It's whether I handle 14,505 feet specifically, which is 2,000 to 3,000 feet above anything I've been on foot. The research on this is clear: VO2max drops roughly 1.9 percent per 1,000 feet, submaximal heart rate climbs 10 to 20 beats while max heart rate drops, and fitness does not prevent acute mountain sickness. The only thing that does is time at altitude. The plan for the day or two before the summit bid is to camp at elevation, which takes the AMS variable off the table as much as it can be.

The Six-Pack peaks handle the altitude build up to around 11,500 feet. The last 3,000 feet to the Whitney summit is the gap that only the summit day itself can close.

## The Ankle

I sprained my left ankle on April 5th running down from Pyles Peak. My foot hit a rock wrong on the descent, outside of the foot catching the ground and the inside facing the sky, and I heard it pop through my AirPods over the music. I'd always had sturdy ankles — years of hiking and rec soccer without a meaningful injury — and this was different. I finished the run anyway and even did a six-mile walk later that day with no extra pain. The swelling came over the following two days.

Two more incidents followed. A tweak on the Wilson descent that hurt for a couple of minutes and then resolved, with some swelling over the next day or two. And a roll on an uneven crack in the pavement across the street from my house on a run, which was more painful than the original injury, bad enough that I couldn't walk normally for the rest of that day. All three incidents on the same left ankle, each landing differently.

Today it held through Cucamonga and Ontario without issue. I'd put it at 90 percent, the remaining piece being residual swelling that shows up the day after heavy effort, not any limit on the actual movement. I'm more deliberate about foot placement on technical terrain than I used to be, which is probably a net positive going into the Sierra.

## Between Hikes

The running runs on a parallel track through August, separate from the hiking schedule and for a different purpose. The target is 30 to 35 miles a week: one harder session on Tuesdays, easy aerobic miles the rest of the time, with the weekend trail day being wherever the hike schedule puts me. Hiking counts as the trail day but not as the long run, which has its own separate ladder.

Longest single run so far is 10.2 miles, which landed ahead of the target date. The ladder from there goes to 13.1 miles, then 15 before the end of June. Fifteen was originally on the calendar for late July and moved forward three weeks when the trend data suggested the pace was ahead of the original estimate.

The weekly formula is roughly 80 percent easy and 20 percent harder work. Tuesdays get the intervals, everything else runs at a pace slow enough to keep heart rate below 160, which for me right now is slow enough to feel embarrassing on a flat road, but that's the pace that builds the aerobic base without accumulating debt. The logic is that Whitney's hard problems are musculoskeletal and altitude-related, not purely cardiovascular, and the running builds the cardiovascular floor while the hiking builds the legs-and-feet experience you can't get from road miles.

## What "Ready" Looks Like

I have never been an athlete. Rec soccer as a kid, no varsity anything, no running background. I started hiking regularly in my mid-twenties and started running this year, at twenty-six, with no formal training history and a resting heart rate that gives me some genetic baseline to work with but nothing trained to draw on.

So ready for me isn't a VO2max number. It's a benchmark I've set that maps almost exactly to what Whitney actually asks for: two El Cajon Mountain climbs in a single day. El Cajon is 11.2 miles and 3,589 feet of gain, so doubling it lands at roughly Whitney's mileage and somewhat more than its elevation gain. If I can do that, the remaining variable is altitude acclimation — which has a clean solution in spending time at elevation before the summit — as opposed to fitness, which takes months and can't be rushed. Solve the fitness problem and the altitude problem becomes tractable. That's the logic I'm working inside.

## Forward-Looking

Writing up the High Sierra loop and the Three Trees posts recently, I noticed that the trips I used to think of as the peak of my outdoor experience are on the warmup side of where this goes. Half Dome, whenever I did it, was the hardest thing I'd been on. It's less than half of Whitney by distance. That adjustment in scale is uncomfortable but accurate.

The list in my terminal has a longer checked column than it did in January and a shorter open one above it. The diamond next to Whitney is still a long way up the page.
